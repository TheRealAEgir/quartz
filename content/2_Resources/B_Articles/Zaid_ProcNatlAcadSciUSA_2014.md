## Notes

> [!notes]
> %% begin Notes %%**Summary** : This article used microscopy to observe TRM in skin. It describe that TRM have a dendritic morphology, and are located in the basal membrane of the epidermis. They only migrate latteraly in a random manner, which make them not disperse after resolution of inflammation. They interact with LC, but depletion of LC does not impact morphology, number and CD69/CD103 expression, though it impact migration speed. DETC are present in a mutually exclusive way to TRM, have less interaction with TRM. AHR is important to TRM persistence as AHR KO have the same initial number of TRM but decline with time. > 
>**Experiment** : gBT-I and HSV infection model
>**Results** : TRM with dendritic morphology in the basal epidermis in contact with basement membrane, visible using SHG fluoresence for collagen distinction. They only migrate latterally, in contrast to gd T cells. Also in hair follicles.
>**Other Results** : Migration is faster when inflammation but slower than dermis. Inflammation does not change the morphology. Speed is approximately five time their 2D dimension per hour.
>
>**Other Results** : There is more TRM at a site of previous infection. There is no dispersion with time.Through modelling, it is hypothetized that the migration is random without need of barrier.
>
>**Other Results** : There is regular interaction of TRM with LC (about half of TRM at any given point). LC density is not affected by TRM density.
>
>**Experiment** : Depletion of LC with DTR-Lg before memory establishement
>**Results** : There is no need for LC in TRM establishement because depletion did not affect TRM number nor CD69/CD103 expression. Same morphology, but migration was slower when depleted.
>
>**Experiment** : CXCR6gfp/- mouse to detect DETC (dendritic epidermal gd T cells)
>**Results** : DETC have larger and stable dendrites. There is less interaction with TRM, and TRM can migrate through space occupied by DETC. There was an inverse correlation between TRM and DETC number, especially at lesion site, suggesting that TRM replace DETC after inflammation.
>
>**Experiment** : Co-transfer of WT/AHR KO effector T cells in mouse injected with DNFB
>**Results** : Similar CD8 T cell entry in skin, but AHR KO declined over time, suggesting a role in persistance.

> > [!Abstract]-
> >
> > Tissue-resident memory T cells (TRM) form in the skin where they are retained and can protect against subsequent infection. Using a combination of intravital imaging and mathematical modeling of skin TRM that form after cutaneous herpes simplex virus 1 infection, we reveal that these memory T cells persist at the site of infection for the life of a mouse owing to slow random migration. We also report that TRM compete with dendritic epidermal γδ T cells in skin for local survival signals, suggesting that T cells compete for space within an epidermal niche., Barrier tissues such as the skin contain various populations of immune cells that contribute to protection from infections. These include recently identified tissue-resident memory T cells (TRM). In the skin, these memory CD8+ T cells reside in the epidermis after being recruited to this site by infection or inflammation. In this study, we demonstrate prolonged persistence of epidermal TRM preferentially at the site of prior infection despite sustained migration. Computational simulation of TRM migration within the skin over long periods revealed that the slow rate of random migration effectively constrains these memory cells within the region of skin in which they form. Notably, formation of TRM involved a concomitant local reduction in dendritic epidermal γδ T-cell numbers in the epidermis, indicating that these populations persist in mutual exclusion and may compete for local survival signals. Accordingly, we show that expression of the aryl hydrocarbon receptor, a transcription factor important for dendritic epidermal γδ T-cell maintenance in skin, also contributes to the persistence of skin TRM. Together, these data suggest that skin tissue-resident memory T cells persist within a tightly regulated epidermal T-cell niche.
